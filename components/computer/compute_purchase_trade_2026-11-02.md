Assumptions: the $400 cap buys a complete mini PC with RAM/SSD; robot power conversion is separate. Heavy learned vision, free conversation, model training and batch processing may use the existing desktop over Wi-Fi. Basic commands, navigation, ordinary picking and safety remain usable locally. The D435 is already owned, so its incremental purchase cost is $0. Lidar, arm, wrist camera, display, audio, battery and general robot wiring are outside this compute/controller/future-camera budget. If Teensy is already owned, subtract $31.50 from cash still to spend; it remains in all system totals.

The purchase is driven by CPU latency and useful headroom. The current implementation does not turn Jetson TOPS into faster Nav2, Python executors or arm kinematics automatically. The RealSense D4 performs the camera's stereo depth calculation; the host still processes depth, point clouds, images and ROS messages. The existing Pi 4 report is unusually useful evidence. `arm_report_C2.json` records MANUAL/TIDY/PATROL at 3.49/3.61/3.60 cores, with at least 2,682 MB available RAM and zero swap. PATROL's Nav2 container used about 103% of one core, arm_node 77%, EKF 35%, mission 33%, MCU link 26% and scan preparation 20%. The later arm-tier-only G1 report, after the bus-wait fix, used about 2.10 cores and retained 2,885 MB available. These are different configurations, not additive costs. They indicate CPU/scheduling pressure rather than a memory-capacity failure.

Those runs used a PC simulation at approximately 0.7× real time and omitted real D435 capture, active voice and tags. They do not establish the physical robot's utilization at wall-clock speed. A Pi 5 is a credible candidate for a tuned, slower profile; its performance is not established by the Pi 4 failure. Conversely, neither extra RAM nor a second Pi guarantees that a saturated navigation callback meets its deadline. Sources: [C2 report](../../characterization/pi_camera/results/arm_report_C2.json), [G1 report](../../characterization/pi_camera/results/pi_arm_G1_arm_tier_nav_off_with_bus_wait_fix_192546.json), [design §4.10](../../design.md#410-pi-in-the-loop-real-ball-benches-paused).

Use 16 GB on a mini PC; buy 32 GB when the premium is small or when developing/building on the robot. Eight GB is a plausible operating capacity for this non-neural stack; 16 GB provides room for camera buffers, models, browser/debugging and builds. Four GB is adequate as a candidate camera-head budget with bounded queues, not a reason to buy an expensive 16 GB Pi. Large RAM cannot accelerate CPU-bound navigation.

For catching, reserve a stiff common bar with about 0.30–0.40 m optical-centre baseline, subject to CAD, collision and field-of-view checks. This is a proposal, not changed camera geometry. The current D435's roughly 50 mm stereo spacing is useful for the near field and is not an equivalent long-range colour stereo pair. A third centre RGB camera also does not make monochrome depth eyes into two colour eyes.

An illustrative pinhole calculation shows the value of baseline. At 1280 px width, 80° horizontal FOV and 7 m, focal length is about 763 px. Assuming independent **disparity error of 0.25 px**, depth-error propagation is `sigma_Z = Z² sigma_d / (f B)`:

| Baseline B | Disparity at 7 m | Approximate depth sigma |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.05 m | 5.4 px | 32 cm |
| 0.10 m | 10.9 px | 16 cm |
| 0.30 m | 32.7 px | 5.4 cm |
| 0.40 m | 43.6 px | 4.0 cm |

These are calculations, not measurements or D435 specifications. They omit calibration bias, lens distortion, exposure blur, correspondence errors and occlusion. At these optics a 74 mm ball is only about 8 px across at 7 m; making the baseline wider does not enlarge it. Relative yaw error of 0.1° is about 1.3 px at this focal length and can overwhelm the assumed subpixel noise. A rigid bar, stable manual focus, repeatable calibration and short exposures matter at least as much as compute. Excessively wide spacing hurts near-field overlap and packaging; retain the D435 for picking.

With OAK, select the **RVC2 4P with V1 OV9782 modules** for this 120 fps candidate: current RVC4 OV9782 documentation lists 60 fps full resolution. A newer platform can have a slower driver mode. [Luxonis sensor/driver table](https://docs.luxonis.com/hardware/sensors/OV9782).

At 10 m/s, a ball moves approximately 17 cm between frames at 60 fps, 11 cm at 94 fps and 8 cm at 120 fps; those are sampling gaps, not exposure blur. At 2 ms exposure it moves 2 cm during the exposure. A global shutter removes line-by-line skew but not this blur or the need for enough light. The previous Pi 4 one-camera 120 fps measurement used a rolling-shutter Module 3, 768×432 ISP output and no ball; it does not qualify either new pair. Preserve the design's exposure/latency targets and judge actual 74 mm-ball detection when catching is authorized to resume.

Do camera detection on the Pi head and send stamped detections/track covariance over short Ethernet, with clock mapping and MCU trigger sequence maintained. Two 1280×800×120 streams are 246 MB/s even at one byte/pixel, or 737 MB/s as RGB888, excluding protocol overhead. Gigabit Ethernet cannot carry those raw pairs. USB 3 at 5 Gbit/s also cannot carry the RGB888 pair at that rate. Use appropriate on-device scaling/ROI and output format, bounded latest-frame queues, or faster verified transport. Do not infer low latency from advertised 120 fps. A 10 Gb/s camera board helps only if host ports/cables and the pipeline support it.

OAK's ISP/VPU can reduce host image work; the existing C++ blob detector can run on the mini PC on appropriately sized streams. Porting the whole detector to OAK is optional development, not an assumed existing feature. Dense colour stereo depth at 120 fps is also not promised by a 120 fps sensor. For the ball, detect each image then triangulate sparse observations.

Desktop offload should accept images/crops plus time/pose metadata and return optional semantic decisions with deadlines. Proposed buying sequence:

1. Choose the compact M5 Ultra / M8 class after the size constraint: M5 offers RAM expansion and lower height; M8 has a smaller footprint, another fast USB-A and USB4 but fixed RAM. M5's quoted $420/16 GB/256 GB remains a candidate, not an adopted purchase. Include Teensy, hub/interface/power and the owned D435; add no catch camera now. Fernando excluded the earlier business Tiny recommendation by size.
2. Qualify the physical arm/navigation stack with actual D435, lidar, face, speech and console. Acceptance should include completed navigate-then-tidy tasks, busiest-thread/aggregate CPU, callback/data-age tails, free RAM, thermal throttling and DC watts; no simulations were started for this trade. Aim for meaningful reserve (e.g. sustained total CPU under roughly 60–70%, no critical thread continually saturated), but accept on task/deadline evidence rather than that heuristic alone.
3. When catching resumes, first specify field of view, 0.30–0.40 m bar feasibility, short-exposure indoor signal, frame rate and exposure-to-detection age. Budget about $500 for either Pi/Arducam or RVC2 OAK/OV9782. Prefer the OAK candidate if 120 fps and host independence matter; prefer Pi if its wider stock view and direct control of custom capture/detection are more useful. Validate the entire synchronized pair, not sensor advertising. If 120 fps is a hard requirement, the documented Pi/Arducam 94 fps mode and full-resolution IMX296 60 fps mode do not meet it.
4. Add onboard CUDA only after a named local neural workload fails its deadline on the mini PC and desktop offload is unsuitable. A future Jetson can be a vision coprocessor, retaining the x86 machine, or replace it after measured power/latency tradeoffs. **Synchronization has three different jobs.** First, synchronize the two camera exposures electrically: Teensy pin24 fans out through the correct voltage translation to both camera trigger inputs. CSI, USB, ROS message pairing and Ethernet clock sync do not substitute for that common trigger. Pin26 can observe an exposed sensor strobe; without access to a real exposure indication, trigger-to-exposure delay is a camera calibration item. The OAK FSIN route and signal direction depend on board revision/socket group; use the [FFC hardware documentation](https://docs.luxonis.com/hardware/platform/deploy/ffc), not a generic header pin guess. Only one circuit drives a trigger net.

Second, map MCU time to the main computer. Both Python and C++ MCU links implement `host_time = a × mcu_time + b` using USB command/state echoes; GPIO pulse timestamps can improve the fit. On a mini PC with no exposed GPIO, **USB echo is the available existing fallback**. It preserves event timestamps and estimates drift, but asymmetry remains unknown. Fit residual is not proof of absolute accuracy; the older <50 µs pulse target is not established by echo mode. A USB-FTDI GPIO/DCD dongle does not automatically restore that precision: the [Linux PPS documentation](https://www.kernel.org/doc/html/v6.14/driver-api/pps.html) explicitly discusses USB-induced latency/jitter.

For the arm, qualify echo mode with real D435/lidar/odometry and keep the MCU in charge of deadlines. For future precision catching, select a measured timing path: Pi/Orin edge capture plus verified intercomputer clock mapping, or a true PC UART DCD/LinuxPPS path if the exact PC has a supported native serial option and proper electrical conversion. The latter needs a PPS reader integration, not an FTDI cable. Current Orin HTE-capable input pin/pinmux remains VERIFY. A proposal should not claim precision timing closure from the presence of a 40-pin connector.

Third, align PC/Pi Linux clocks for ROS data. Start with chrony over the short wired link and measure offset under load; Wi-Fi is for desktop offload. If tighter alignment is required, hardware-timestamped PTP/NTP must be supported by both exact NICs/drivers: verify `ethtool -T`, do not infer it from 1/2.5 GbE speed. Hardware camera trigger-sequence/MCU timestamps should be retained through the pipeline so network arrival time does not become exposure time. A Pi observer could receive pin25 on a free GPIO and forward edge observations after explicit clock conversion; the observer/conversion is not implemented. GPIO18/header12 is only a possible reservation, subject to the exact CamArray HAT pin use. Sources: [chrony](https://chrony-project.org/examples.html), [kernel timestamping](https://docs.kernel.org/networking/timestamping.html).

The D435 has its own sensor clock, mapped by librealsense/ROS to host time; verify timestamp domains/global-time behaviour for the installed driver. No external D435 sync wire is allocated here. A 30 Hz depth trigger would need its own compatible interface and must not simply be attached to the catch pair's 120 Hz trigger. A2M12 exposes a scan stream without a usable absolute acquisition-clock/PPS interface in this design; reconstruct sample times from scan timing, calibrate delivery delay and de-skew with IMU/odometry. Arm status is stamped on host read; this is not a servo hardware capture timestamp. Wrist UVC timing similarly requires driver/latency characterization if used during motion.

Actual implementation gaps found by inspection:

- `omni_camera/libcamera_capture.cpp` currently maps SensorTimestamp from its assumed monotonic clock to ROS time. It does **not** consume trigger TIME events or use `FrameStamper`; verify the installed libcamera timestamp domain and exposure semantics before precision use.
- `camera_head.launch.py` launches **one** capture instance. Two independent CSI cameras need two captures; Arducam's concatenated stereo frame needs an explicit split/paired-image path. Neither is established by the mono bench result.
- `FrameStamper` exists as a core but is not integrated into that hardware capture path. OAK needs its DepthAI capture/time/trigger bridge too.
- Firmware's current camera pulse is a **20 µs active-high** pulse. Official IMX296 XTR controls exposure by **active-low pulse width**; it is not directly compatible with this output. Each selected camera needs the appropriate trigger polarity/width and voltage interface, without blocking the 1 kHz control loop for millisecond exposures. [Pi GS external trigger](https://www.raspberrypi.com/documentation/accessories/global-shutter-camera.html).
- Hardware transports exist in the MCU/arm nodes, but the current common bring-up still creates simulation TCP endpoints; A2M12 and the real D435/USB/udev setup need an explicit physical launch. Existing software support is not a completed physical deployment.

### Resource and performance trade

The existing robot workload is principally CPU-limited: Nav2/MPPI, localization, ROS scheduling, point-cloud/filtering and ordinary arm vision use CPU. The D435 produces depth internally. Sixteen GB provides camera/model/debugging buffer capacity; RAM expansion matters for future local models and builds, not for accelerating a saturated CPU. TTS and command recognition add CPU peaks. GPU benefit requires a selected implementation/backend; none provides CUDA. Radeon 780M is the strongest graphics option here, but gaming performance does not predict ROS speed. Noncritical neural/batch tasks can stay on the desktop over Wi-Fi.

Illustrative primary public Geekbench 6 records (not these three units tested together): [8745HS, unidentified chassis, Linux 6.4](https://browser.geekbench.com/v6/cpu/13080666): 2522 / 12357 single/multi; [i5-13420H, Lenovo laptop, Ubuntu 6.3](https://browser.geekbench.com/v6/cpu/9284673): 2071 / 7558; [actual M5 Ultra, Windows Balanced 6.7.1](https://browser.geekbench.com/v6/cpu/18312649): 1806 / 7312. Different OS, version, cooling and power limits prevent turning these into robot speedups. They support the BMAX CPU class as the stronger reserve choice; they do not establish FPS, robot CPU percentages or battery runtime. Getorli H-series versus M5 U-series is not a measured whole-system efficiency comparison.

All three have this baseline port allocation: D435 on direct fast A; Teensy on A using native USB CDC and a micro-B data cable (no FTDI); SO-101 on A through its half-duplex TTL servo-bus adapter; A2M12 on A through its USB/UART and motor-power adapter. C remains spare for USB audio, wrist camera or service access. Serial adapters and servos need appropriate separate power; do not feed servo/motor power from PC USB. If arm/lidar kits do not include adapters, add their actual prices before ordering. HDMI face video and separate regulated display power consume no additional data socket. A powered USB hub is optional expansion, particularly if audio and a wrist camera both use USB. Keep D435 direct.

Analog speaker/mic use requires actual microphone, compatible splitter, powered speaker/amp and software echo handling; a jack is not a built-in microphone/speaker. M5 CTIA support is documented; other two need confirmation or a USB audio dongle (allow $15). No speech-quality guarantee follows from jack presence.

Teensy supplies robot CAN/GPIO/UART and hardware timing. Existing USB echo maps MCU timestamps to PC time for the arm; this has no measured <50 us guarantee. Later GS stereo requires a common hardware trigger, voltage-compatible interface, exposure/frame association and measured cross-computer clock alignment. Connect a future Pi camera head over Ethernet and send detections; a second NIC is convenient but not essential. NIC speed and count do not prove hardware PTP timestamping. Wi-Fi offload is suitable for noncritical tasks.
